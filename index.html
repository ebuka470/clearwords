Not sure where to begin? Follow this. It works whether you're learning for yourself or with your kids.

Day 1–2

Do one Scene

Pick the person you most want to talk to: a grandparent, an auntie, a partner's family. Do a Scene about greeting them.

👨‍👩‍👧 Parents: do it together. Let your child hear the phrase first.

Day 3–4

Save five Moments

Choose five words you'd really use: greetings, thank-you, please. They become flashcards for a minute of review.

🌱 Learners: pick the words you've been avoiding.

Day 5

Say it to a real person

One greeting, out loud, to someone who speaks it. A voice note or a call counts. Effort is what people notice.

Weekend

Practice together

Keep your streak, try a second Scene, join a pod or find a language partner for company. Small and steady beats big and rare.

Parents • Tips

Why Your Child Understands but Won't Speak (and What Helps)

6 min read

You speak to them in Yorùbá, Igbo or Pidgin. They answer in English. You may worry you've failed. You haven't. Understanding before speaking is the normal first stage for heritage speakers, and you can help them cross over.

Why it happens

  • School and friends happen in English, so it's the easy, automatic choice.
  • Speaking out loud feels risky. Kids don't want to get it wrong in front of you.
  • If you understand their English anyway, there's no need to switch.

Five gentle things that help

  1. Shrink the ask. Aim for one word in a real moment, like Ẹ ṣé (thank you) at the table, not full sentences.
  2. Make it a game. Greeting contests, snack-time phrases, a family word of the day.
  3. Repeat, don't correct. If they say it wrong, say it back correctly with a smile and keep going.
  4. Give a real reason. A call with Grandma, a visit home, a cousin who speaks it. Purpose beats pressure.
  5. Keep it short and daily. Five minutes most days beats a long weekend lesson.
Ẹ káàrọ̀ oGood morning (respectful, Yorùbá)
Ẹ ṣé oThank you (respectful, Yorùbá)
DaalụThank you (Igbo)
AbegPlease (Pidgin)

🤓 Timmy's tip

Celebrate the attempt, not the accent. A kid who feels safe saying a word wrong will say it again tomorrow.

Greeting Elders in Yorùbá: The Respectful Basics

5 min read

In Yorùbá culture, greeting an elder properly is a sign of good upbringing. The good news: a few phrases cover most situations, and people appreciate the effort far more than perfection.

🤓 Timmy's tip

The little Ẹ at the start is the respectful "you". Use it with elders and anyone you want to honor.

The core greetings

Ẹ káàrọ̀ oGood morningEh-kah-roh oh
Ẹ káàsán oGood afternoonEh-kah-sahn oh
Ẹ káalẹ́ oGood eveningEh-kah-leh oh
Ẹ ṣé oThank youEh sheh oh
Ẹ kú ilé oGreetings to the householdEh-koo ee-leh oh
A dúpẹ́We give thanksAh doo-peh

Manners that go with the words

Many families expect a bow or kneel (men often prostrate, women often kneel) when greeting elders. Customs differ between families and regions, so watch what your relatives do and follow their lead.

With an elder, it's best to greet first, then let them lead the "how are you" conversation. Accept food and drink graciously and say Ẹ ṣé o.

Yorùbá • Pronunciation

Yorùbá Tones: A Simple Guide (No Panic)

5 min read

Yorùbá is a tonal language: the pitch of a syllable can change the meaning of a word. That sounds scary, but you don't need to master it before you start speaking.

The three tones

  • High (marked with an acute accent, like ó): a higher pitch.
  • Mid (usually left unmarked): your normal speaking pitch.
  • Low (marked with a grave accent, like ò): a lower pitch.

Same letters, different words

ọkọhusband
ọkọ́hoe
ọkọ̀vehicle

How to not overthink it

  1. Context rescues you. People usually understand from the situation, even if a tone slips.
  2. Listen first. Learn each word with its audio, so the tone comes with it.
  3. Learn phrases, not just words. Tones sit naturally in a greeting you've heard many times.

🤓 Timmy's tip

Mistakes with tones become family jokes. Keep going. Your ear improves faster than you think.

Igbo • Culture

Igbo Wedding Words and Manners (Igba Nkwu)

5 min read

The traditional Igbo wedding, Igba Nkwu, is a joyful, layered ceremony involving both families. You don't need fluency to take part warmly. A few words go a long way.

Words to know

Igba NkwuTraditional wedding (wine-carrying)
UmunnaExtended family / kindred
Ịkụ akaKnocking, the formal introduction
Mmanya nkwuPalm wine
NnọọWelcome
DaalụThank you
Kedu?How are you?

Simple etiquette

  • Greet the older people first and say Nnọọ to those who welcome you.
  • Say Daalụ when offered food, drink or kola.
  • Ask a trusted relative about the specifics. Customs vary between communities and families.

🤓 Timmy's tip

Bring patience and snacks. Igbo weddings are long, lovely and full of people who will cheer your effort.

Nigerian Pidgin 101: Starter Phrases

4 min read

Pidgin connects people across Nigeria. It's relaxed, expressive and everywhere: markets, music, group chats. Here's a starter pack.

How far?What's up?
I deyI'm fine / I'm here
AbegPlease
WahalaTrouble
No wahalaNo problem
OyaLet's go / come on
Na so?Is that so?
I no sabiI don't know
You don tryWell done
ChopEat / food

When to hold back

  • With elders, unless they use it first.
  • In formal settings, unless everyone else is.
  • At traditional ceremonies, where the home language is expected.

🤓 Timmy's tip

Read the room. Pidgin among friends is warm. Pidgin at the wrong moment can feel too casual.
